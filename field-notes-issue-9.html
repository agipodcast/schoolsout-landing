Education 2040.

What should students still be able to do when the tool changes?

Start with the lesson already on your desk.

Today's conversation kept coming back to a sixth-grade geometry project. A model, a protractor, a compass. If AI can help produce the model, what exactly are we asking the student to learn?

That question deserves more room than a yes-or-no argument about AI. I'm proposing four places to examine the work: the capability we want, the evidence we accept, the boundaries around the tool, and the teacher's next move. Education 2040 is a name for that work. It is a framework to try and challenge, not a prediction of what school will become.

These are fictional classroom situations with authored consequences. Change your mind, compare the alternatives, and carry one experiment into your own planning. There is no score.

The wrong future / Grade 6 geometry

Name what survives the tool.

A class designs a model shelter. Before choosing the materials or software, name the capability: reasoning about shape and space, explaining a design, or accurately using a particular instrument. Each deserves a deliberate place in the assignment.

Select any combination. These describe a capability from different angles; they are not bins for sorting children or subjects.

Foundational: spatial reasoning helps students make sense of later mathematics and design.

Change the model

The roof gets taller. What changes?

A symmetrical triangular roof, viewed from the end. Predict the change in the base angles before moving the ridge. The span stays 6 units.

BASE ANGLES 34° eachWHAT STAYS FIXED 6-unit span

Angles are rounded. Students can measure a drawing; calculating them with trigonometry is not the sixth-grade task here.

Protractor and compass proficiency are candidates for the time-bound lens. That does not make them obsolete. If the goal is angle measurement, the instrument matters. If the goal is spatial reasoning, examine whether changing tools preserves that reasoning.

02
The impressive artifact / Grade 6 ELA

Give understanding another chance to show up.

A polished essay about The Tell-Tale Heart arrives. An explanation and a prompt history arrive with it. AI can help produce all three. Plan several moments where the student interprets, connects evidence, and responds when the reading becomes less comfortable.

Inspect the assignment

How does the ending change the opening?

Ask the student to cite a specific detail from the opening and another from the ending, then explain the connection. Use the class's full text. The cards below are paraphrases for this scenario.

OPENING / PARAPHRASE

The narrator insists on being sane and presents heightened senses as evidence.

ENDING / PARAPHRASE

The narrator becomes convinced the officers hear the sound too, then confesses.

What could AI contribute here?

Offer a competing interpretation: perhaps the narrator's careful planning complicates a simple claim that nothing they say can be trusted. The student must decide what that reading explains, what it misses, and which details support the response.

The teacher selects the passage and checks the competing reading against the text. An AI interpretation is material to examine, never the answer key.

Plan what you will observe

Keep the dimensions separate.

Use these levels to rehearse your rubric. They do not grade the fictional student. In class, collect evidence across moments and record the support provided.

Offer accessible ways to respond: discussion, annotation, drawing with explanation, or supported writing. A timed oral defense should not become the only route to demonstrating understanding.

This gives us more useful evidence of understanding. It cannot prove that a student read every word, establish authorship, or catch every misuse of AI.

Try the same move in social studies or design thinking

Nile settlement: students explain a location choice using agriculture and trade. Then introduce a failed flood. Which assumption breaks? What would they change, and why?

Design thinking: identify the problem and stakeholders, investigate what people need, set success criteria, design with AI where appropriate, explain decisions, test, and iterate. The next constraint should require a new judgment from the student.

03
The governance gap / A district decision

Make the boundary usable.

A district already uses Google Classroom. A teacher wants AI-supported rehearsal. Begin by asking whether an existing enterprise provider meets the need. A familiar relationship can reduce the burden of continually shopping for tools. The actual service still needs district review.

Build the review

What has the district actually checked?

Select the evidence available in this fictional situation. These checks do not authorize a real product.

What the decision exposes

A familiar name leaves questions open.

A district review should cover the specific product, account type, age group and configuration. This example makes no claim that any particular Google AI service is approved for a sixth-grade class.

A large vendor does not guarantee protection from loss. A logo cannot substitute for an agreement, working controls, or an exit you have actually tried.

04
The expert trap / Grade 6 Spanish

Step in. Then give the work back.

Teams invent a café, create a menu, and rehearse ordering in Spanish. An approved AI tool can help with menu ideas or a practice partner. The teacher sets language goals, checks the output, establishes boundaries, and watches for the gap that needs instruction.

Coaching depends on subject expertise. The teacher needs to recognize the language problem, choose an explanation that fits, and hear whether the next attempt improves. Mastering every changing menu tool is a different demand.
