Turn Your Glasses Around

A practical conversation about the AI-infused future.

By Jason Padgett

I have been thinking about glasses a lot lately. The obvious reason is that I am wearing a pair of Meta glasses in the picture above. The less obvious reason is that they have become a pretty good symbol for the conversation America keeps having about AI.

Some people put on a pair of AI glasses and see a cure-all. The thing will fix work, school, healthcare, loneliness, government, dinner plans, and possibly the fact that their inbox has become a small municipal landfill. Other people look through the same lenses and see a looming harm. Surveillance. Job loss. Environmental cost. A faster, less human world.

Both reactions are understandable. Neither one is enough.

There is an old story in recovery literature about a man realizing that he had been wearing his glasses backwards. The world looked wrong because he was looking through the wrong end of the lenses. I keep coming back to that image when I watch us argue about AI, wearables, and the infrastructure that makes any of it possible.

Acceptance Is Not Surrender

Accepting that we are headed into an AI-infused future does not require us to accept every product, every policy, every data center proposal, or every company’s terms of service. It means we stop pretending that denial is a strategy.

That matters because the future is already showing up in ordinary places. It is showing up in the glasses people wear, in the tools people use at work, in the systems that quietly decide what gets prioritized, and in the compute infrastructure being debated in towns across the country. You can dislike pieces of that future. You can demand better guardrails. You can tell a company to take a hike. Those are all available moves.

What we cannot do is act surprised that the road exists after traffic has already started moving on it.

Let’s be real. Americans are not simply privacy purists. Most of us carry a phone that knows where we are, what we buy, who we talk to, and what we searched for at 1:14 in the morning. What makes smart glasses feel different is that the technology becomes visible in a human interaction. It is on someone’s face. You can see the possibility of capture, assistance, translation, or distraction sitting across the table from you.

That visibility is useful. It forces us to ask better questions.

Questions Worth Asking

What does this add to a person’s life or work? What does it subtract? Who gets the benefit? Who carries the cost? Who controls the data? What would make the tool trustworthy enough for the situation we are actually in?

Those are better questions than cheering every new announcement, and they are better than treating every new device as a five-alarm fire. They help us separate the practical from the theatrical.

A wearable that helps someone navigate a city, hear a conversation more clearly, translate a language, remember an instruction, or get through a hard workday is not the same thing as a system built to extract attention and data. A data center that supports local capability, jobs, and reliable infrastructure is not automatically a good deal, either. The answer depends on the terms, the accountability, and whether the people affected have a real voice in the decision.

When we focus only on the problem, the problem grows until it takes up the whole frame. When we focus on a practical solution, the solution has room to grow too. That does not mean we become gullible. It means we keep our agency.

Let’s Build Better Rooms

I do not think the next useful conversation happens only online, with one group selling magic and another group warning that the sky is falling. We need rooms where people can bring a real problem, compare notes, show what they have tried, and leave with something they can use on Monday morning.

That is why Harrison Painter and I are exploring AI Fusion, a practical Midwest AI conference shaped by the people who would actually attend it. We are asking what kinds of sessions people want, what problems they are trying to solve, and what would make a conference worth leaving home for.
