Now Make It Useful

Seeing AI clearly is the first move. The next move is turning a messy workday into smaller decisions a person can actually use, check, and own.

By Jason Padgett

SourceAn end-of-day workflow
PatternBig work becomes small decisions
Human roleTruth, importance, and safety

The workday

One messy pile. Several useful things.

I have built a habit of saving my end-of-day workflows as simple HTML pages. I call them Sophomore build records because I want them clear enough that my high-school sophomore daughter could open one, understand what happened, and use it to present the work to a class.

That standard forces me to explain the work instead of hiding behind a polished output. On August 15, the page had to make sense of competing plans, training notes, a large to-do list, project records, and several decisions hiding inside all of it. That is what I mean when I say, “We turned a messy workday into things people could use.”

AI helped sort the pile. The useful outputs were modest: two follow-up pages, a few audience-specific views, clearer project tickets, and one short page with three questions.

Let’s be real. Nobody needs another forty-page AI answer sitting beside the other forty-page AI answers. People need to know what is true, what matters now, and what decision unlocks the next piece of work.

AI earns its place in the workday when it makes the next human decision easier without pretending to make that decision for us.

Four moves from the record

How the pile got smaller.

Each move created something a real person could open, question, and use.

01

Compare the plans before choosing one.

Several AI agents were collaborating in a Slack channel, looking at the same work, and producing different plans for it. The disagreement was useful because it exposed the choices. A polished plan is still a proposal. The human job is to compare the plans, question their assumptions, correct the facts, and choose what matters.

Evidence

The build record preserved the competing plans instead of treating the first one as the answer.

Useful move

Ask the AI to label assumptions, unknowns, tradeoffs, and the decisions only a person can make.

02

Make training useful after everyone leaves.

A two-day workshop could have disappeared into a folder full of notes. Instead, the material became two simple review pages: one explained the larger model, and one helped people practice the conversation they would need to have next.

The team’s feedback was straightforward. The two pages made the workshop material easier to understand and gave them something useful to return to after the session.

Evidence

Two shareable HTML pages turned a completed session into something people could revisit.

Useful move

End training with one reference page and one practice page built around the work people will actually do.

03

Give different people different views.

In K-12 education, differentiation starts with a simple truth: one presentation does not help every student in the same way. Adults deserve the same consideration at work. A complete project board helps the people steering the work, while an individual path helps one person see the next move without sorting through everything else.

Evidence

The same source material produced a project view, a personal path, and a smaller decision page.

Useful move

Ask who will use the information, what they need to decide, and what can safely stay out of their view.

04

Check the confident answer against today.

Problem: One AI plan described tools and workflows that sounded completely reasonable but were no longer being used. The answer was organized. It was also out of date.

Solution: Run a reality check that compares the confident answer against current evidence. Notice when an answer sounds complete, then verify it against the way the work is actually done today.

What the check caught

Human review found the outdated tools and workflows before the plan became an instruction.

Useful move

Mark the facts that can expire, then verify the important ones against today’s evidence and with the person who owns the work.

Reality check

Organized can still be wrong.

This is the skill I want more people to practice. Notice when an answer sounds complete but does not match the room, the system, or the work as it exists today. Give the AI better context. Keep unknowns visible. Put a human checkpoint where a wrong assumption could waste time, money, or trust.

Before you use the output

  1. Which facts could have changed?
  2. Who owns those facts now?
  3. What decision will this output influence?
  4. What happens if the answer is wrong?

The human takeaway

AI organizes and explains. People decide what is true, important, and safe.

Try this tomorrow

Give AI a smaller job.

What messy part of your workday would become easier if AI returned three small decisions instead of one giant answer?

  1. Choose one pile of notes, tasks, or plans that is slowing you down.
  2. Name the person who will use the result and the decision they need to make.
  3. Ask AI for the smallest useful view, page, or question set that supports that decision.
  4. Check every important claim against current reality before anyone acts.
