Give the second attempt a job.

In The Receipt Is the Learning, I asked what a student could do with less help. That piece credited my conversation with Bryon Haverstick from Sagamore Council. Here is a proposed adult-team rehearsal for educators to adapt: keep one skill in view, change one condition, and make room to explain what happened.

First, name the skill

What should the person be able to do?

For an invented café conversation, the goal might be asking for an alternative when an item is unavailable. An educator should choose language and expectations appropriate to the learner.

The first attempt

Make the help visible.

Offer a short example or a phrase card. Let participants rehearse and note what they used. An AI assistant can play a role if the tool and material are approved; another adult or a printed exchange works too.

One changed condition

The usual order is unavailable.

Keep the setting familiar. Change the available item and ask the participant to respond. Avoid piling on a new topic, a time limit and unfamiliar vocabulary all at once.

A fair boundary

Keep necessary supports.

Reducing a practice hint is a choice to make carefully. Preserve accessibility supports and accommodations. Use fictional details; do not upload identifiable student work to an unapproved tool.

A small record can help the team discuss the two attempts without guessing where the thinking happened.

Attempt

What did you try?

Keep the response, including the awkward part. A polished rewrite can hide the choice worth discussing.

→

Explanation

Why that response?

Ask what changed and how the participant adapted. Leave room for an honest request for help.

→

Next practice

What would help now?

Choose the next useful hint or example from what you observed. The educator makes that judgment.

One successful retry is a small piece of evidence. It does not establish durable learning or prove that AI caused improvement.

Let the explanation stay a little messy.

A participant might manage the changed order and still struggle to explain the words they chose. Someone else might stumble, notice the problem, and ask exactly the right question. Those are different things to work with.

My call is to keep the response and the explanation together. Let's be real, a beautiful answer can make the adults feel finished before we have learned much about what the person can do.

What changed in the task? What changed in the response?

Start with adults.

Try the invented exchange with a colleague before adapting it for students. Check whether the changed condition actually calls on the skill you named.

Inspect the AI role.

If an assistant participates, check its language and behavior first. Giving away the response or changing the task midstream can spoil the rehearsal.

Use the result to plan.

Keep notes brief: support offered, response attempted, explanation given, next practice. An educator can decide what deserves another look.

Make one useful change.

This is a proposed exercise, not a reported classroom trial. Choose one familiar practice task and write its second version beside it. If you cannot explain what the change is meant to reveal, revise the task before asking a learner to do it.

The reply question

What would you change in a familiar task to see whether the learning travels?

Keep the skill clear and the next step small.
